Charing Cross Station & The Strand

🚶Next: York Steps (~280m • ~4 mins)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
Braun & Hogenberg Map (1572): Commercial City vs. Royal Westminster
Old Charing Cross (Engraving published by N. Smith, 1792)
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • A Binary Metropolis: In 1485, England had a population of only 2.2 million, with around 50,000 living in London. By 1603, London quadrupled to over 200,000, creating two distinct hubs: the commercial City of London to the east and the royal court at Westminster to the west.
  • The Urban Mortality Deficit: London was an epidemiological deathtrap. With contaminated wells and open sewage gutters, deaths constantly exceeded births. It only expanded because thousands of young rural migrants arrived every year for apprenticeships.
  • Epidemic Shocks: Late summer 1485 brought the first outbreak of the Sweating Sickness, killing 15,000 Londoners in six weeks and forcing Henry VII to postpone his coronation.
  • The Eleanor Cross: Built by Edward I in the 1290s to prompt Catholic prayers for his late wife in purgatory. Puritans destroyed it in 1647 as idolatrous; the current cross is an 1865 Victorian Gothic revival monument.
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: Visual Analysis & Architectural Style
Examine Barry's 1865 Eleanor Cross forecourt replica:
  • Formal Elements: Identify the vertical gothic features: canopied niches, crocketed pinnacles, and carved heraldic shields.
  • Ways of Seeing: How does this 19th-century Victorian Gothic monument attempt to invent historical memory in front of a modern Victorian railway terminus?
⚖️ Politics Task: Geography of Power
Observe the junction between the Strand (leading to the City) and Whitehall (leading to Parliament):
  • The Constitution: Why was it politically significant that the monarch’s government at Westminster was physically separate from London’s financial capital in the City?
  • How does this ancient division foreshadow the concept of the separation of powers?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Did London's explosive population growth strengthen the Tudor monarchy through taxable trade, or weaken it by creating an uncontrollable city that rivalled the Crown?

York Steps & Watergate (The Strand Palaces)

🚶Next: Charles I Statue (~420m • ~6 mins)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
Stuart-era Map showing aristocratic palaces and watergates along the Strand
Moonlight view of York Watergate resting directly on the River Thames
19th-century engraving of the York House Watergate and river stairs
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • Expropriation of Church Wealth: Before 1536, the south side of the Strand was lined with grand town palaces belonging to wealthy Catholic bishops.
  • Redistribution to Courtiers: During the Dissolution of the Monasteries, Henry VIII seized these properties and handed them to courtiers (e.g. Somerset, Leicester, Burghley) to buy political loyalty.
  • York House Lineage: Originally Norwich House, Henry gave it to his brother-in-law Charles Brandon. It later became York House, home to Queen Elizabeth’s Lord Keepers, including Francis Bacon.
  • Thames Highway & The Clean West End: The Strand was often impassable mud. The river was the primary highway for noble state barges. Because prevailing winds blew smoke eastward toward the City, the West End developed as an aristocratic enclave.
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: Classical Orders & Rustication
Examine the stone York Watergate (built c. 1626, attributed to Inigo Jones or Nicholas Stone):
  • Architectural Language: Identify the heavy rusticated Tuscan columns and the arched central portal.
  • Baroque / Renaissance Motifs: Look at the carved scallop shell crest of George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham. How does this river entrance use Italian classicism to display aristocratic grandeur?
⚖️ Politics Task: Property Rights & Royal Prerogative
Consider the expropriation of these palaces:
  • Constitutional Limits: When Mary I returned England to papal obedience in 1553, Parliament accepted Catholic doctrine but refused to return seized Church lands.
  • What does this reveal about the power of private property to limit royal prerogative?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Did seizing and redistributing Church property give the Tudor Crown absolute control, or did it make monarchs permanently dependent on the landed gentry?

Equestrian Statue of Charles I (Trafalgar Square South)

🚶Next: Palace of Whitehall (~220m • ~3 mins)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
Canaletto, Northumberland House (1752) — Note the Charles I equestrian statue on its pedestal at right
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • A Bronze Survivor: Cast in 1633 by Hubert Le Sueur, this was England's first Renaissance equestrian statue. During the Civil War, Parliament ordered it melted down, but a metalsmith buried it in Holborn. Charles II erected it here in 1675.
  • The Great Chain of Being: Tudor and Stuart society rationalized inequality through the Great Chain of Being. Rebellion against social superiors was condemned as a sin against God.
  • Paternalism and Deference: Less than 1% of the population owned over a quarter of England's land. In exchange for elite protection and grain in lean years (paternalism), commoners offered obedience, bowing, and stepping aside in the street (deference).
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: Equestrian Typology & Renaissance Authority
Look up at the bronze statue of Charles I facing down Whitehall:
  • Classical Prototype: How does this draw on Donatello’s *Gattamelata* and Roman imperial bronzes of Marcus Aurelius?
  • Iconography: Notice how the King sits in total composure, commanding the beast with light reins. How does this symbolize reason mastering the unruly body politic?
⚖️ Politics Task: Divine Right vs. The Rule of Law
Observe the statue staring towards the Banqueting House (his execution site):
  • The Prerogative: Charles claimed absolute rule under the Divine Right of Kings.
  • How did the eventual execution of the King establish the modern constitutional principle of the Rule of Law?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Was social order in early modern England held together by genuine religious respect for hierarchy, or by the threat of violent state punishment?

The Palace of Whitehall (Northern Approach)

🚶Next: Banqueting House (~110m • ~2 mins)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
Fisher’s Ground Plan of Whitehall (1680) showing the maze of courts and royal apartments
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • Seizure from Wolsey: In 1529, when Cardinal Wolsey failed to secure the annulment of Henry VIII’s marriage to Catherine of Aragon, the King stripped him of York Place under Praemunire.
  • Europe's Largest Palace: Renamed Whitehall, Henry transformed it into a 23-acre royal hub with 1,500 rooms, replacing the fire-damaged Palace of Westminster as the monarch’s principal London residence.
  • Spatial Filtering: Power in a personal monarchy depended on physical proximity to the King. Whitehall was organized in strict chambers: Great Hall $\rightarrow$ Presence Chamber $\rightarrow$ Privy Chamber.
  • The Heart of Dynasty: Henry VIII married Anne Boleyn (1533) and Jane Seymour (1536) at Whitehall, and died here in January 1547.
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: The Architecture of Magnificence
Inspect the sprawling layout shown in Fisher’s 1680 ground plan:
  • Domestic Display: Notice how Tudor palaces replaced fortified military walls with decorative red brick courtyards, large mullioned windows, and recreational spaces (tiltyards and real tennis courts).
  • How was Renaissance *magnificentia* (splendour) used to intimidate visiting ambassadors?
⚖️ Politics Task: Household vs. Institutional Executive
Consider the layout of government offices:
  • Cabinet Government: Under Thomas Cromwell, informal power held by household servants (like the Groom of the Stool) was reorganized into an institutional Privy Council.
  • How does the evolution of the royal court at Whitehall parallel the modern UK executive and Cabinet?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Did concentrating government power inside the King’s private bedchamber make the Tudor state more decisive, or dangerously corrupt?

Stop 5

The Banqueting House

🚶Next: Execution Site (~25m • <1 min)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
Peter Paul Rubens, The Apotheosis of James I (Central Oval Ceiling Canvas, 1636)
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • An Architectural Revolution: Completed in 1622 by Inigo Jones for James I, this was England's first purely classical building, replacing older timber and brick Tudor halls.
  • Rubens's Ceiling: In 1636, Charles I installed nine monumental canvases painted by Flemish master Peter Paul Rubens.
  • Apotheosis of James I: The central oval canvas depicts Charles's father being carried up to heaven by allegorical figures of Justice, Truth, and Peace, visually celebrating absolute Stuart monarchy.
  • The Irony of History: On 30 January 1649, Charles I was led directly beneath these paintings on his way out of an opened window to the execution scaffold.
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: Classical Orders & Baroque Allegory
Examine the stone exterior of the Banqueting House:
  • The Classical Orders: Identify the lower Ionic pilasters and upper Corinthian columns. Notice the alternating triangular and segmental pediments over the windows.
  • Baroque Illusion: Contrast Jones’s calm, restrained Palladian architecture with Rubens’s dramatic Baroque ceiling inside. How does Rubens use dynamic angles and rich color to overwhelm the viewer?
⚖️ Politics Task: Court vs. Country Conflict
Consider the political impact of this building in the 1630s:
  • Royal Prerogative: How did Charles I fund lavish court culture during his 11 years of Personal Rule without calling Parliament?
  • Why did Puritan MPs view this building's Italian design and Rubens ceiling as evidence of Catholic, absolutist tyranny?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Can monumental art and architecture genuinely persuade people to accept a king's authority, or does excessive royal display always trigger rebellion?

The Execution Site of Charles I

🚶Next: Holbein Gate Site (~80m • ~1 min)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
Contemporary Broadsheet Print depicting the public execution outside Banqueting House (1649)
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • The Morning Walk: On Tuesday, 30 January 1649, Charles I walked from St James’s Palace across the park to Whitehall, asking for two shirts so the winter cold would not make him shiver and seem afraid.
  • The Scaffolding Theatre: A scaffold draped in black cloth was built right outside the first-floor windows of the Banqueting House, surrounded by soldiers to keep back the crowd.
  • The King's Final Speech: Charles looked out and asserted: "A subject and a sovereign are clean different things." Laying his head on a low block, he was beheaded with a single clean blow.
  • A Groan from the Multitude: An eyewitness noted there was "such a groan by the thousands then present as I never heard before." The execution smashed the sacred belief in the Great Chain of Being.
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: Broadsheets & The Cult of Martyrdom
Study the contemporary print of the execution:
  • Visual Narrative: Notice the composition: the raised scaffold, the masked executioner, and the sea of spectators beneath.
  • Martyrdom Iconography: How was this catastrophic visual event counteracted by Royalist prints (such as the *Eikon Basilike* frontispiece) showing Charles trading his earthly crown for a crown of thorns?
⚖️ Politics Task: The Rupture of Sovereign Immunity
Stand where the scaffold was erected:
  • Constitutional Turning Point: For centuries, the law held that the monarch could do no wrong. Parliament broke this by trying and executing their own king for high treason.
  • How did this act pave the way for modern parliamentary sovereignty established in the 1689 Bill of Rights?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Was the execution of Charles I a lawful trial establishing that rulers are accountable to their people, or an illegal military coup?

The Site of the Holbein Gate

🚶Next: Henry VIII Wine Cellar (~110m • ~2 mins)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
George Vertue’s 1725 Engraving of the Holbein Gate (demolished 1759)
19th-century woodcut depicting the secret marriage of Henry VIII and Anne Boleyn
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • Bridging Court and Leisure: Built by Henry VIII in 1532 across the muddy public road, this gatehouse linked the eastern riverfront apartments with western tiltyards and tennis courts without the King having to walk in the foul street.
  • The Secret Marriage: Before dawn on 25 January 1533, Henry VIII secretly married Anne Boleyn in an upper chamber here. Secrecy was critical: Henry was still legally married to Catherine of Aragon, but Anne was already pregnant with Elizabeth.
  • Break with Rome: To declare this marriage legal and ensure his child's legitimacy, Henry and Thomas Cromwell passed the 1533 *Act in Restraint of Appeals*, declaring England an independent empire subject to no foreign pope.
  • Demolition: The gate survived the 1698 palace fire but was torn down in 1759 to widen Whitehall for modern traffic.
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: Tudor Gothic meets Florentine Renaissance
Examine George Vertue’s 1725 engraving of the gate:
  • Gothic Foundations: Spot the battlemented parapet, octagonal corner turrets, and chequerboard flint-and-stone patterns.
  • Florentine Accents: Look closely at the circular terracotta portrait medallions of Roman emperors, sculpted by Pietro Torrigiano. Why did Henry combine traditional English castle design with fashionable Italian Renaissance ornaments?
⚖️ Politics Task: Statute Law & The Imperial Crown
Consider the legal steps taken to validate the secret marriage held here:
  • Statute Power: Instead of relying only on royal proclamation, Henry used Acts of Parliament to dissolve Church power in England.
  • How did this establish statute law as the highest authority in the land?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Was the English Reformation an act of national independence by the English state, or an act of personal desperation by a monarch seeking a male heir?

Henry VIII’s Wine Cellar

🚶Next: Great Hall Site (~70m • ~1 min)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
Henry VIII’s vaulted Tudor brick wine cellar, preserved deep beneath the MoD
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • Elite Hospitality: Constructed in 1536 under Wolsey’s original brick vaults, this cellar measured 70 by 30 feet to supply massive royal feasts.
  • Gascony Claret: Whitehall received over 300 massive tuns of wine every year shipped from Gascony up the Thames. Because fresh wine soured quickly, cellarmen sweetened it with spices and sugar to make *hippocras*.
  • Class and Diet: While courtiers drank imported wine and ate sugar-heavy feasts, ordinary laborers drank ale for hydration and ate beans and coarse bread. Elite sugar consumption caused rotting black teeth, seen in portraits of Elizabeth I.
  • Preserved by Engineering: In 1949, engineers moved the entire 800-ton brick cellar 19 feet down and 9 feet west on steel rails so the modern Ministry of Defence could be built around it.
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: Brick Vaulting & Imported Craftsmanship
Examine the photograph of the interior cellar:
  • Vaulting System: Identify the low ribbed vaults supported by short octagonal stone piers.
  • Material Status: In early Tudor England, fine brick masonry was an imported prestige technique from the Low Countries, reserved for royal palaces before becoming a common building material.
⚖️ Politics Task: From Royal Household to Civil Service
Stand outside the Ministry of Defence main building:
  • Executive Evolution: How did an estate originally built for royal entertaining and feasting transform into the administrative headquarters of the modern defense state?
  • Consider the concept of executive dominance in modern UK politics.
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Did the extreme inequality in diet and luxury between the Tudor court and the working population undermine the moral claim that the monarchy governed for the good of all?

The Site of the Great Hall (Whitehall)

🚶Next: William Tyndale Statue (~270m • ~4 mins)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
Reconstruction drawing of the Tudor Whitehall palace riverfront and Great Hall
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • The Court Networking Machine: Before the Banqueting House was built, this hall was the public stage of court life where gentry networked, sought offices, and competed for marriage contracts.
  • Shakespeare at Court: On 1 November 1611, Shakespeare’s The Tempest had its first recorded performance here before King James I, celebrating the restoration of natural order and legitimate princely rule.
  • Sir Thomas Smith’s Four Sorts: In the 1560s, Tudor writer Sir Thomas Smith divided England into Gentlemen, Citizens, Yeomen, and the Fourth Sort who 'have no voice nor authority in our commonwealth.'
  • Common Dignity: Keith Wrightson’s court records reveal commoners took pride in their self-reliance: one 1589 craftsman declared he was worth 'a couple of pence, if every bird had his feather' (he owed no one anything).
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: The Optical Politics of Staging
Consider how court entertainment was staged inside a Great Hall:
  • Perspective Staging: In Jacobean court masques designed by Inigo Jones, single-point linear perspective was arranged so the illusion only resolved perfectly from the King’s royal chair.
  • How does this technique make the monarch the optical and ideological center of the universe?
⚖️ Politics Task: Factions, Lobbying & Patronage
Observe the courtyard where courtiers waited for entry:
  • Patron-Client Networks: Tudor monarchs managed regional power by granting trade monopolies and titles to loyal allies.
  • How does this system of court patronage compare to modern lobbying and political donations in the UK Parliament?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Were ordinary Tudor working people entirely excluded from power, or did local communal networks give them an informal voice that monarchs could not ignore?

William Tyndale Statue

🚶Next: Westminster Hall (~520m • ~7 mins)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
Foxe’s Book of Martyrs woodcut: William Tyndale strangled and burned at the stake (1536)
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • Banned Words: Under medieval heresy laws, translating the Bible into English without permission was punishable by death. Tyndale fled to Germany in 1524 to translate the New Testament directly from Greek and Hebrew.
  • Smuggled Truth: Printed copies were smuggled into London inside cloth bales. Bishop Tunstall bought up copies to burn at St Paul's Cross, which ironically helped Tyndale fund improved editions.
  • Martyr's Prayer: Betrayed and burned at the stake in 1536, Tyndale’s dying words were: 'Lord! Open the King of England’s eyes.' By 1539, Henry VIII authorized the English *Great Bible* for every parish pulpit.
  • Rising Literacy: Keith Wrightson notes literacy among yeomen rose from 45% in 1580 to over 60% by 1610. Reading Foxe’s *Book of Martyrs* created a deeply Protestant national identity.
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: The Graphic Power of the Woodcut
Examine the woodcut illustration from Foxe’s *Book of Martyrs*:
  • Visual Storytelling: Notice the bold linework, the banner containing Tyndale's last words, and the dramatic execution scene.
  • How did cheap woodcut prints function as an accessible visual weapon for ordinary, semi-literate people compared to expensive oil paintings?
⚖️ Politics Task: Freedom of Conscience & The Public Sphere
Stand before the bronze statue of Tyndale with his hand on the printing press:
  • Civil Liberties: How did placing an English Bible in every parish church empower individuals to interpret truth for themselves?
  • How does this relate to modern liberal concepts of freedom of speech and conscience?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Did translating the Bible into English unite the kingdom under a common Protestant identity, or fracture it into permanent sectarian division?

Westminster Hall (Palace of Westminster)

🚶Next: Westminster Abbey (~170m • ~2 mins)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
Bird’s-eye perspective of the medieval Palace of Westminster and law courts
Interior of Westminster Hall showing Richard II's colossal hammerbeam timber roof
Tudor/Jacobean paneled judicial chamber typical of Westminster Palace courts
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • Ancient Seat of Justice: Built in 1097 by William Rufus and re-roofed in the 1390s by Richard II, this hall housed the core common law courts (King's Bench, Common Pleas, Chancery).
  • Treason Trials: The Tudor Crown tried its most dangerous political enemies here: Sir Thomas More and Bishop Fisher (1535), Anne Boleyn’s alleged lovers (1536), and the Duke of Northumberland (1553).
  • Litigation Boom: Keith Wrightson reveals that lawsuits at Westminster jumped from 5,300 in 1560 to 13,000 by 1580, showing that the English gentry were settling property disputes through law rather than private violence.
  • Anne Boleyn’s Feast (1533): Anne sat on an elevated throne served by nobles on horseback, with 32 dishes served in the first course alone, while Henry VIII watched privately from a concealed lattice window.
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: Structural Scale & The Hammerbeam Feat
Examine images of Hugh Herland's timber roof:
  • Structural Innovation: Spanning 68 feet without central columns, cantilevered hammerbeams transfer immense roof weight down through vertical walls.
  • Gothic Iconography: Look at the carved wooden angels terminating each beam: how did this ceiling symbolize heaven watching over royal justice on earth?
⚖️ Politics Task: Judicial Independence vs. State Terror
Consider how trials were carried out in this space:
  • Judicial Tenure: Under the Tudors, judges served 'at the King’s pleasure' and could be fired for ruling against the Crown.
  • How does this contrast with modern judicial independence established by the 1701 Act of Settlement?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Were Tudor treason trials in Westminster Hall genuine exercises of common law, or rigged theatrical spectacles designed to terrify potential rivals?

Westminster Abbey (West Front & Lady Chapel)

🚶Next: The Jewel Tower (~160m • ~2 mins)
🚶 Directions to Next Stop
Engraving of Anne Boleyn’s elaborate royal coronation procession to the Abbey (1533)
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • A Royal Peculiar: Surrendered in 1540 during the Dissolution of the Monasteries, Elizabeth I made the Abbey a Royal Peculiar in 1560, answering directly to the Crown.
  • Tudor Necropolis: Henry VII, Elizabeth of York, Edward VI, Mary I, and Elizabeth I are all buried here (Henry VIII is buried at Windsor). Mary and Elizabeth share a single tomb constructed by James I.
  • Anne Boleyn’s Coronation (1533): Anne walked a 300-yard red carpet from Westminster Hall. Six months pregnant, she wore purple velvet and kept her hair down like a virgin bride, and was crowned with St Edward's Crown—a relic normally reserved for reigning sovereigns.
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: The Italian Renaissance in Tudor England
Consider the interior of the Lady Chapel (Henry VII Chapel):
  • Collision of Styles: Look at the English late-medieval fan vaulting juxtaposed against Pietro Torrigiano's bronze-and-marble tomb of Henry VII and Elizabeth of York (1518).
  • Florentine Naturalism: How do Torrigiano’s classical putti, acanthus scrolls, and naturalistic facial sculpting bring Italian High Renaissance humanism into England?
⚖️ Politics Task: The Coronation Oath as Contract
Stand facing the West Front:
  • Constitutional Compact: The coronation ritual requires the monarch to swear an oath to uphold the laws of the realm and maintain the Protestant Church of England.
  • How does this traditional service reflect an uncodified constitutional contract between Crown and citizens?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Does the grand sacred spectacle of a coronation create real political stability, or is it merely political theatre masking vulnerability?

The Jewel Tower (Abingdon Street)

📍Tour Complete: The Jewel Tower
📍 View Current Location
Cutaway architectural diagram showing the three stone vaulted levels of the Jewel Tower
Aerial view of the Jewel Tower situated beside the medieval Palace of Westminster
Step 1: Lead Historian Brief (Tudors 1C)
  • Medieval Survivor: Built in 1365–1366 by Edward III as a private royal treasury with its own moat, this is one of only two complete buildings from the medieval Palace of Westminster to survive the fire of 1834.
  • From Jewels to Wardrobes: When Henry VIII moved the court to Whitehall, the tower lost its status; an inventory of 1547 shows it held clothes, furniture, and dolls belonging to Princesses Mary and Elizabeth.
  • Birth of the Paper State: By 1600, it became the repository for the Clerk of the Parliaments. Its fireproof stone vaults stored all original manuscript Acts of Parliament from Henry VII's reign down to 1864.
Step 2: Specialist Fieldwork Inspection
🎨 Art History Task: Utilitarian Vernacular & Defense
Inspect the stone exterior of the tower:
  • Materials & Form: Note the rough Kentish ragstone walls, Reigate stone dressings, and simple trefoil-headed windows.
  • Functional Contrast: Contrast this fortress-like medieval masonry with Inigo Jones’s symmetrical Banqueting House up the road. How do materials reveal the changing purpose of royal buildings?
⚖️ Politics Task: Statute Law & Constitutional Memory
Consider the records stored inside this tower:
  • Written Precedent: All Acts passed by King-in-Parliament were stored here.
  • How did having an unshakeable written archive of past laws empower Parliament to resist the arbitrary power of Charles I?
Step 3: Synthesis Street Debate
Street Debate Prompt

Which contributed more to modern British democracy: violent armed civil war, or the gradual accumulation of written laws preserved in parliamentary archives?
